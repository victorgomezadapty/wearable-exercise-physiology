The average describes almost nobody.

The group result says a mental-stress task raises heart rate by +5 bpm. A physiotherapist or coach decides about one person. So this tab asks a practitioner's questions: did this person respond beyond their own measurement noise, through which channel, how many tests would you need to know, and how hard did each person actually work?

From group effects to individual decisions

Sports science has a standard toolkit for judging one person (Hopkins 2000; Atkinson & Batterham 2015; Swinton et al. 2018). Everything below is computed per participant on the stress day, from quality-checked 30-s windows, using the same TMCT − seated-baseline contrast as the group analysis.

Typical error

2.4 bpm

How much one person's heart rate moves between two comparable seated rests on the same day, with nothing happening. This is the noise floor for any individual judgement.

Smallest worthwhile change

2.3 bpm

0.2 × the between-person SD at rest. The noise is as large as the smallest change worth detecting. That is the core difficulty of monitoring individuals.

True individual differences

SD 7.6 bpm

The spread of responses left after removing measurement noise (95% CI 5.3–9.3). People genuinely differ: about one in four has no true HR rise, and about one in four rises by more than 10 bpm.

Who really responded?

Each row is one person: stress-task response (dot) with a 90% interval (line) built from the typical error. Clear response means the whole interval is above the smallest worthwhile change (dashed line). No meaningful response means it is entirely below. Everything else is unclear.

How many tests would you need?

Averaging k repeated tests shrinks the interval by √k. The curves show when the 90% half-width falls below the smallest worthwhile change, i.e. when one person's response can be judged with confidence.

Interval half-width relative to the smallest worthwhile change

Below the line, measurement noise is smaller than the change worth detecting.

Practical translation. To call a client a "high stress reactor", measure them on several occasions, or with longer stress exposures, and compare against their own baseline, never against a population threshold. One short test is a hypothesis, not a label.

Same stressor, different channels

Each point is a person. Heart-rate and skin-conductance responses are unrelated across people, so a monitor that watches only one channel misses about half of the people with a clear response.

HR response vs EDA response

Dashed lines = smallest worthwhile change on each axis. Click a point to open that person's card.

External load is not internal load

The cycling protocol prescribes cadences, not intensities. Expressed as a percentage of each person's age-predicted maximum heart rate (208 − 0.7 × age), the same protocol meant very different things for different people, when the wrist could measure it at all.

Relative intensity per participant

Bands = ACSM categories. A final-stage value is reliable with ≥ 3 valid 30-s windows and HR not below the moderate stages; unreliable values are shown, not removed.

Only 14 of 30

aerobic participants have a final-stage wrist HR a practitioner could use. For the rest, the PPG failed or produced values below the easier stages.

Age-predicted HRmax is rough

Individual error is about ±10 bpm (≈ ±5 percentage points). When a decision depends on it, use a measured maximum or RPE.
